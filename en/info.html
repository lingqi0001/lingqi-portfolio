Computer Engineering / Electrical Engineering (CE / EE)

I did not start with hardware or electrical engineering. For most of high school, I saw myself much more clearly in User Experience, Human-Computer Interaction, and software development. I liked starting with a simple questions like why is something frustrating to use? Then I could watch how people behaved, rethink the interface or workflow, and build something that made the experience a little better.

CHSchat.xyz became one of the projects where that way of thinking developed the furthest.

It began as a communication platform for Mr. VW and his students at Centennial High School, but over time, I realized the disconnected workflows between tools. I wanted to bring together parts of school life that normally existed across different websites, tools. I worked with Mr. Boring and Mr. Bartholomew to plan this into a platform that include school and club information, events, messaging, Eagle Time sign up, a grade calculator, cafeteria menu info, community tools and developer tools for future extensions created by others.

But even something as basic as signing in became a design constraint. It was not enough to build an authentication page that worked on my own computer; the system had to fit the Microsoft accounts students actually use through HCPSS.

Working on CHSchat taught me that making a better product and getting that product to work in the real world are very different problems.

On a screen, I could keep improving almost anything. I could redesign a page, change a workflow, add a feature, or even customize part of the platform around the needs of a particular teacher. But once I began thinking seriously about adoption beyond my own project, the problems changed.

A school may already have years of data stored in an existing platform. A better interface does not make that data easy to move. Different schools depend on different account systems, devices, and technical infrastructure. At Centennial, Chromebooks and school accounts are already part of everyday academic life. But when I thought about how the same kind of product could work in schools in China, I realized that I could not simply assume that every student would have an individual Chromebook or another computer available whenever a lesson required one.

At that point, the problem was no longer how to design a better interface, but becoming What if the user does not have the right device for that interface in the first place?

That question pushed me toward hardware.

I began experimenting with inexpensive classroom response devices and e-ink communication tools. Instead of assuming that a school should first build an entire device ecosystem around my software, I became interested in doing the opposite: designing a simple physical device around one real need and making it inexpensive enough that deploying it could actually make sense.

My current hardware prototypes still rely heavily on off-the-shelf microcontrollers, displays, and other modules. I can connect those pieces, write the software, and make a prototype function. But that experience has also made the limits of what I currently know much more visible.

There is a large difference between assembling modules until a prototype works and designing a product that is compact, reliable, inexpensive, power-efficient, and realistic to manufacture. To cross that gap, I need to understand what is happening underneath the modules I am using: circuit design, PCB design, electronic components, embedded systems, power, communication, firmware, and the way hardware and software are designed together.

That is why moving toward Computer Engineering or Electrical Engineering does not feel like leaving HCI behind.

It feels more like following the same problem one layer deeper.

I started by asking how an interface could work better. CHSchat made me ask how an entire system could fit into a real institution. Hardware then made me ask what kind of device should exist underneath that system in the first place.

Computer Engineering especially interests me because it sits directly at the boundary between software and hardware. Electrical Engineering interests me because it would give me a much deeper understanding of the circuits, electronics, and physical systems that I currently know mostly through finished modules.

Both would give me the part of the product-development process that I am still missing.

My goal is not to leave User Experience behind and become an engineer who only cares whether a circuit meets its specifications or whether a piece of code runs.

I want to keep the way I already approach problems: start with the person, understand what is actually getting in the way, and then decide what technology should be built around that problem.

CHSchat was important to me because it taught me how much can be improved by reorganizing a confusing digital experience around the people who actually use it. At the same time, it taught me that usability alone does not determine whether a product succeeds. Cost matters. Existing infrastructure matters. Device availability matters. Compatibility matters. Data migration matters. A teacher's willingness to change a workflow they have used for years matters.

Sometimes the best solution may be better software.

Sometimes it may require a different device entirely.

In college, I want to develop the engineering knowledge that would let me make that second choice seriously. I want to study circuits, PCB design, embedded systems, computer architecture, electronics, firmware, and hardware-software integration, rather than treating the physical side of my projects as a collection of finished components that I simply connect together.

Long term, I want to be able to follow a problem much further than I can today.

If a teacher needs a simpler way for an entire class to respond at once, I do not want my only answer to be another website. If a school does not have enough personal computing devices to support a piece of software, I do not want the project to end with, “Then they cannot use it.”

I want to be able to keep designing.

Maybe that means changing the communication method. Maybe it means reducing the number of components. Maybe it means designing a custom board, lowering the cost, changing the physical interaction, or building a device that removes the need for a general-purpose computer altogether.

I am less interested in building technology that is impressive because it is complicated than in building technology that survives contact with the real world.

A classroom device that a school can actually afford interests me. A tool that fits into a teacher's existing routine instead of forcing that teacher to rebuild everything around it interests me. A product that a student can pick up and understand almost immediately interests me.

Ultimately, I want to bring together three parts of my background that I currently understand at very different depths: the human-centered thinking I developed through UX and HCI, the ability to turn ideas into working systems through software, and the deeper control over physical technology that I hope to gain through Computer Engineering and Electrical Engineering.

I want to be able to start with:

“Why is this so difficult to use?”

and keep working until I can ask:

“What would we have to redesign to make it work?”
